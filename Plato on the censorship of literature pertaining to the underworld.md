What does Socrates argue must be censored from literature pertaining to the underworld and why does he argue it
#### Notes
**Key Propositions and Claims**
- **The Re-valuation of Hades:** Poets must praise the domain of Hades rather than disparage it to ensure the youth choose death in battle over slavery (386b–c).
- **Expunging the Lexicon of Fear:** Specific "terrible and fearful names" must be deleted: Cocytus, Styx, "those below," and "the withered dead" (387b–c).
- **The Prohibition of Heroic Lamentation:** Deletion of verses depicting Achilles or Priam—a near offspring of the gods—wailing, rolling in dung, or entreating others (388a–c).
**Assumed Premises (Unargued)**

- **The Aesthetic Paradox:** Aesthetic excellence in poetry increases the danger of its psychological impact; beauty acts as a conduit for the "shiver" that softens the soul (387b–c).

**Argued Premises (Demonstrated)**

1. A "decent man" believes that death is not a terrible thing for another decent man (387d).
2. The decent man is uniquely self-sufficient for living well and has the least need of others (387e).
3. Therefore, such a man will bear the loss of a son, brother, or fortune with the most gentleness and the least lamentation (387e).
4. Consequently, the city must not allow renowned men to be depicted wailing, as this would make the guardians unable to resist doing things similar to those such people do (388a).'

The educational program for the guardians begins with a strategic censorship of the afterlife, a move necessitated by the psychological requirements of military fortitude. Socrates posits that if the youth are to be courageous and "fear death least," the city must reconstruct the soldier’s hierarchy of fears: slavery and defeat must be viewed as far more terrible than the transition to Hades. This theological regulation is not concerned with the "truth" of poetic beauty; indeed, the more "poetic and sweet" a verse is, the more dangerous its impact on those intended to remain free (387b). By removing the dread associated with the underworld, the state ensures that the guardians do not suffer a physiological weakening—becoming "hotter and softer" than they ought (387c) through the shivering induced by fearful imagery.
