What remains to be defined regarding speech about human beings as opposed to gods and heroes (392a)?
#### Notes
Narrative tradition regarding heroic greed and divine misbehavior is "neither holy nor true"; it causes the young to be "sympathetic with themselves when they are bad" by offering them divine precedents for vice (390d–391e).

**Key Propositions/Claims:**

- Achilles must not be portrayed as a "lover of money" or "full of confusion" (391a–b).
- Tales of "terrible rapes" by sons of gods must be rejected as impossible (391c–d).

- **Assumed Premises:**
    - Axiom: Evil cannot be produced by gods (391e).
- **Argued Premises:**
    - If youth hear that the "close relations of gods" (391e) commit terrible deeds, they will excuse their own badness.
    - Logos vs. Lexis: Once the content (_Logos_) is purified, the _Lexis_ (mode of speech) must be addressed because the _manner_ of speech is itself a form of soul-shaping.
- **Connective Tissue:** With the content of speech closed, Socrates shifts to the psychological impact of _mimesis_ (imitation) on the soul’s unity.

The "purging" of the heroic narrative aims to prevent a "proclivity for badness" in the youth. Socrates highlights the logical contradictions in the poetic tradition, such as Achilles possessing "two diseases that are opposite to one another"—illiberality (money-loving) and "arrogant disdain" for gods (391c).
