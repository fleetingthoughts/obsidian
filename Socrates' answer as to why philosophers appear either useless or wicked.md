In response to Socrates' third wave of arguments from Book 5, Adeimantus rebuts that philosophers are often wicked or useless (487b). State Socrates counterargument.
#### Notes
Two criteria:
	- How the many corrupt the philosophic soul.
	- What causes philosophy to be filled with incompetent imitators.
- Socrates uses the analogy of the Ship of State (488a–489c)
- Socrates places the blame on the masses by process of elimination since we accept the arguments that philosophers are suited to rule (489b)
The argument as to why philosophers appear either useless and incompetent or wicked is split in two parts:
	- How the many corrupt the philosophic soul.
	- What causes philosophy to be filled with incompetent imitators.
- **The Corruption (491a–495a):** Socrates argues that the most vigorous and best natures, when given a bad rearing, turn out "exceptionally bad". The multitude acts as the ultimate sophist, using the uproar of assemblies and the threat of death or fines to corrupt the young philosopher and force him to adopt their corrupted morals.
- Socrates argues that philosophers recognize the corruptive power of the masses and that the pursuit of knowledge is separate from persuasive powers, they are therefore compelled to impose self-exile. Because philosophy is a respected practice, this allows imitators to take up its mantle and give philosophy a bad name.
