How does Socrates logically dismantle the popular claim that epic and tragic poets possess true knowledge (_episteme_) of all human crafts, virtues, and divine affairs? (Book 10, 599a–600e)
#### Notes
**Argued Position.**<br>• _Premise_: If a person possessed true knowledge of both the real things themselves and their imitative phantoms, they would be far more serious about producing real deeds (the originals) rather than crafting mere imitations to leave behind as memorials (599a–b).<br>• _Argument_: Socrates examines Homer's life and shows he left no real-world evidence of practical wisdom: he cured no sick like Asclepius (599c), established no political constitutions or laws like Lycurgus or Solon (599d–e), won no wars (599e–600a), invented no ingenious technical devices like Thales (600a), and founded no educational way of life like Pythagoras (600a–b). Therefore, it is argued that Homer and other poets do not possess true knowledge, but are merely "imitators of phantoms of virtue" (Book 10, 600e–601a).
