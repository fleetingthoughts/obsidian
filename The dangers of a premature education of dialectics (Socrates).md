Why is the premature study of dialectic dangerous for young students, and how does the analogy of the "changeling child" explain this?
#### Notes
- **The Changeling Child:** The young student, raised securely with ancestral convictions about what is just and fair (538c).
    
- **The Discovery of Adoption:** The student experiences refutation in dialectical argument, realizing his ancestral laws are not absolute truth (538d-e).
    
- **The Flatterers:** Opposing, pleasure-seeking practices that draw the destabilized soul toward them (538d).
    
- **Conclusion:** Deprived of his old convictions and lacking true knowledge, the young man becomes an outlaw, treating arguments like a game to tear at others like a puppy, bringing slander upon philosophy (539a-c)

**Argued Position.**<br>• _Premise_: Young students who taste dialectic too early misuse it as a game of contradiction, refuting others like playful puppies and falling into deep skepticism (Book 7, 539b–c).<br>• _Argument_: Just as a changeling child reared in wealth ceases to respect their adoptive parents once they discover they are not their biological parents, yet cannot find their real parents (Book 7, 537e–538c), a young student who is suddenly refuted by dialectic will abandon their ancestral moral convictions without finding the true ones (Book 7, 538d–539a). Therefore, it is argued that dialectic must be forbidden to the young and reserved only for older, stable natures (Book 7, 539c–d).
