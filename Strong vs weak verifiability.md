Why does Alfred Ayer reject "strong" (conclusive) verifiability as a criterion of factual significance, and what is its devastating logical consequence?
#### Notes
**Reductio ad Absurdum Argument**: Refutation of conclusive verificationism.<br><br>**Definition of Strong Verification**: A proposition is verifiable in the strong sense of the term if, and only if, its truth could be conclusively established in experience beyond any possibility of doubt.<br><br>**Ayer's Refutation (Premises & Conclusion)**: <br>1. _Premise 1_: General propositions of law (laws of nature, e.g., "arsenic is poisonous," "all men are mortal," "bodies expand when heated") are designed to cover an infinite or indefinite number of cases.<br>2. _Premise 2_: No finite series of observations can ever conclusively establish an infinite number of cases. Thus, general laws can never be strongly verified.<br>3. _Conclusion_: If we adopt strong verifiability, we must logically dismiss all scientific laws of nature as meaningless nonsense, alongside metaphysics. Because this stultifies science, strong verifiability is self-stultifying as a semantic criterion.<br><br>**Citation**: Chapter 1, Pages 36–37.
