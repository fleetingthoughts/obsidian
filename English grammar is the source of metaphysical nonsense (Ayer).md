How does the grammatical structure of Indo-European languages mislead philosophers into postulating the metaphysical concept of "substance"?
#### Notes
**Logical Analysis of Language**: Grammatical category mistake.<br><br>**Grammatical Mechanism**: In ordinary language, we cannot refer to the sensible properties of an object (e.g., "the table is round, brown, and hard") without introducing a single grammatical subject word ("the table") that appears to stand for the thing itself, distinct from its properties.<br><br>**Metaphysical Error (Assumption)**: Philosophers infected by the primitive superstition that "to every name, a single real entity must correspond" assume there must be an underlying, unobservable entity ("substance" or _substratum_) that "holds" or "has" these properties.<br><br>**Ayer's Phenomenalist Correction (Logical Definition)**: Logical analysis reveals that what makes various sense-experiences "appearances of the same thing" is not their relation to an unobservable underlying substance, but their logical relations to one another. "Substance" is a logical construction out of sense-contents, but grammar misleads us into treating it as a metaphysical entity.<br><br>**Citation**: Chapter 1, Page 42.
