#### Notes
**1. Oneness vs. Apparitional Multiplicity (475e–476a)**

Socrates explains that while opposite concepts exist in pairs (such as fair and ugly, justice and injustice, or good and bad), **each Form by itself is strictly one**. However, because these Forms constantly mix and show up in community with physical actions, bodies, and one another, **each Form appears to be many**—creating an "apparitional many" in the sensory world.

**2. Eternal Stability and "In-Itself" Reality (476b–476d, 479a, 479e)**

A Form is a singular, absolute reality that exists "itself"—such as **"the fair itself,"** **"beauty itself,"** or **"justice itself"**. Unlike the shifting, physical world of particulars, a Form is defined as an idea that **"always stays the same in all respects"**.

**3. The Relation of "Participation" (476c–476d)**

Socrates draws a sharp boundary between a Form and the physical things that copy it:

- Particular physical things **participate** in the Form (for example, particular fair things participate in Beauty), but they are not the Form itself.
- Someone who recognizes both the Form itself and its participants without confusing them is cognitively **"awake"**.
- Conversely, someone who believes in beautiful particulars but denies the existence of "beauty itself" is **"dreaming,"** because they mistake a mere likeness or copy for the actual thing it resembles.
