What are the hard and soft criteria Socrates outlines for an appropriate education in preparation for dialectics that leads him to choose the subjects he choose and how one should approach them.
#### Notes
**Socrates explicitly states the criteria in Book 7.** He establishes **two main criteria** that any subject must meet to be included in the higher education of the future philosopher-rulers (521d).

These dual criteria are:

**1. The Primary Criterion: Turning the Soul from "Becoming" to "Being"**

- **The Concept**: The subject must have the intrinsic power to draw the mind away from the physical world of changing, sensory experiences ("becoming") and direct it toward the eternal, unchanging realm of true reality ("being") (521c–d). It must act as "the turning of a soul around from a day that is like night to the true day" (521c), forcing the soul to use pure intellect rather than physical senses to eventually contemplate the Idea of the Good (518c–d, 525a-b).
- **Explicit Evidence**: Socrates asks, _"What then, Glaucon, would be a study to draw the soul from becoming to being?"_ (521d). He later uses this as a strict sorting mechanism for the subjects. For instance, when evaluating geometry, he states: _"Then if geometry compels one to look at being, it is suitable; if at becoming, it is not suitable"_ (526e).

**2. The Practical Criterion: Utility for Warriors**

- **The Concept**: Because these guardians must serve as military leaders in their youth, the subject must have immediate, practical value on the battlefield (521d).
- **Explicit Evidence**: Socrates explicitly mandates that _"the study we are seeking must have this further characteristic... It mustn't be useless to warlike men"_ (521d).
    - **Calculation** is chosen because a general must know how to count, number, and marshal an army; without it, he would be as ridiculous as Agamemnon in the tragedies who did not even know how many feet he had (522c–d).
    - **Geometry** is selected because a warrior needs it for _"pitching camp, assaulting places, gathering the army together and drawing it up in line"_ (526d).

By applying these two criteria, Socrates systematically rejects contemporary disciplines like gymnastic (which only oversees physical decay and growth) and music (which trains through habits and rhythm rather than active intellectual knowledge), while accepting and radically restructuring the mathematical sciences

Beyond the two primary pillars of **turning the soul toward being** and **military utility**, he establishes five additional negative and positive requirements that any candidate subject must satisfy to be included in the guardians' curriculum:

**1. The Non-Commercial Constraint (Not for Trade)**

Socrates explicitly demands that these subjects must not be practiced for commercial, mercantile, or transactional purposes.

- **The Textual Rule**: The guardians must take up calculation "not after the fashion of private men, but to stay with it until they come to the contemplation of the nature of numbers with intellection itself, not practicing it for the sake of buying and selling like merchants or tradesmen" (525b–c).
- **The Rationale**: A study is only useful if it is practiced "for the sake of coming to know and not for trade" (525d).

**2. The Perfection and Completeness Requirement**

Socrates places a strict quality barrier on the mathematical disciplines, banning any corrupted or simplified variations of these sciences.

- **The Textual Rule**: He states "that those whom we shall be rearing should never attempt to learn anything imperfect, anything that doesn't always come out at the point where everything ought to arrive" (530e–531a).
- **The Rationale**: If a science does not always reach the absolute, uncorrupted mathematical truth (such as empirical astronomers who only look at the physical, moving sky), it is an imperfect study and must be rejected (530e–531a).

**3. The Ethical Orientation (The Quest for the Fair and Good)**

A subject cannot simply be a neutral intellectual exercise; it must have a direct vector toward the ultimate moral reality of the state.

- **The Textual Rule**: When examining harmonics, Socrates clarifies that the study is only "useful, rather, for the quest after the fair and the good, but pursued in any other way it is useless" (531c).
- **The Rationale**: If a science is studied merely for technical skill or physical entertainment (like musicians who torture strings to find the smallest heard interval), it fails this criterion (531a–c).

**4. The Mutual Kinship Requirement (Capable of Synthesis)**

The chosen subjects must not remain isolated from one another. They must possess an underlying, structural unity.

- **The Textual Rule**: The curriculum is only valuable "if the inquiry into all the things we have gone through arrives at their community and relationship with one another, and draws conclusions as to how they are akin to one another" (531c–d).
- **The Rationale**: Studying these sciences in isolation is "a labor without profit" (531d). This is why, at age twenty, the chosen guardians must integrate their childhood studies into a comprehensive "overview" to demonstrate their dialectical capacity (537b–c).

**5. The Play Criterion (Learnable Without Compulsion)**

Socrates sets a psychological parameter for how these subjects must be presented in childhood, which acts as a natural sorting mechanism for the guardians' characters.

- **The Textual Rule**: Socrates commands that "the instruction must not be given the aspect of a compulsion to learn... because the free man ought not to learn any study slavishly" (536d–e).
- **The Rationale**: "No forced study abides in a soul" (536e). Therefore, the subjects must be introduced to children through play, which also allows the founders to naturally observe which way each child's soul is natively directed (536e–537a).
