What are the two conventional descriptions of Good that Socrates rebuts before providing his own explanation (505a-505d)?
#### Notes
In Book 6 of the _Republic_, Socrates explicitly identifies and systematically rebuts two dominant contemporary definitions of "the Good" (**505b**):

**The Definition of the Many: The Good is Pleasure (505b)**

- **The Claim**: The common multitude defines the ultimate Good as physical or sensory pleasure (**505b**).
- **Socrates' Rebuttal (505c–d)**: Socrates refutes this definition by pointing out an internal logical contradiction that its proponents are forced to accept.
    1. Those who claim that pleasure is the Good are ultimately forced to admit that there are such things as **bad pleasures** (**505c**).
    2. Because they concede that bad pleasures exist, they are trapped in the absurd position of agreeing that **the same things are simultaneously both good and bad** (**505c–d**). Therefore, pleasure cannot serve as the stable, singular definition of the Good.

**The Definition of the Refined: The Good is Prudence or Knowledge (505b)**

- **The Claim**: The more elegant and "refined" thinkers of the city define the Good as prudence, intelligence, or knowledge (_phronesis_) (**505b**).
- **Socrates' Rebuttal (505b–c)**: Socrates demonstrates that this definition is circular and empty.
    1. When those who hold this view are asked to specify what _kind_ of knowledge or prudence constitutes the Good, they cannot point to a distinct field of study (**505b**).
    2. Instead, they are ultimately compelled to say that it is **"prudence about the good"** (**505b–c**).
    3. Socrates ridicules this circularity, noting that they reproach us for not knowing the Good, yet they define it by using the word "good" itself, speaking as if we already understood its meaning (**505c**).
