How does the grammatical similarity between existential and attributive sentences produce the metaphysical illusion of "Being" or "Existence" as a real attribute, and how does Ayer logically refute this?
#### Notes
**Logical and Grammatical Analysis (Quantification Logic)**: Demolition of the ontological predicate fallacy.<br><br>**The Grammatical Illusion**: The sentences "Martyrs exist" (existential) and "Martyrs suffer" (attributive) have the same surface grammar (Noun + Intransitive Verb), tempting philosophers to assume that "exist" credits martyrs with an attribute called "Being" or "Existence," just as "suffer" credits them with "suffering."<br><br>**Refutation (Logical Deduction)**:<br>1. _Premise 1_: When we ascribe a real attribute $P$ to a subject $S$, we presuppose that $S$ exists.<br>2. _Premise 2_: If "existence" were itself an attribute, then asserting that $S$ exists would be a tautology (as existence is already presupposed in the subject), and asserting that $S$ does not exist would be a self-contradiction.<br>3. _Premise 3_: Existential propositions (e.g., "Martyrs exist" or "Unicorns do not exist") are synthetic and contingent, never tautologies or self-contradictions.<br>4. _Conclusion_: Therefore, existence is not an attribute or predicate. Existence is a logical quantifier indicating that a certain class of descriptions is instantiated in reality ($\exists x (Mx)$), not a property possessed by individuals. Speculation about "Being" or "Nothingness" (e.g., Heidegger) is a grammatical category mistake.<br><br>**Citation**: Chapter 1, Pages 42–43.
