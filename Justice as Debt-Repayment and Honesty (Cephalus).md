What is the primary counter-example Socrates uses to refute Cephalus’s definition of justice
#### Notes
Socrates refutes Cephalus’s definition of justice—defined as "speaking the truth and giving back what one has taken" (or paying one's debts)—using the counter-example of returning weapons to a friend who has gone mad.
The Counter-Example (Book 1, 331c–d)

    The Scenario: If a sane friend deposits weapons with you for safekeeping, but later demands them back after losing his mind (becoming of unsound mind), everyone agrees you should not return the weapons or tell him the unvarnished truth.
    The Logic: Returning weapons to a madman would endanger both him and others. Therefore, strictly returning what you borrowed or telling the whole truth in that situation would produce harm rather than good, proving that giving back what was taken is sometimes just and sometimes unjust.

Because following Cephalus's rule blindly in this case results in an unjust action, Socrates concludes that telling the truth and returning what one owes cannot be the universal definition of justic
