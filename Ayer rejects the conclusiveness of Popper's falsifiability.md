How does Alfred Ayer refute Karl Popper's proposal that "conclusive confutability" (falsifiability) should serve as the criterion of factual significance?
#### Notes
**Epistemological Argument**: Demonstrating the logical symmetry of non-conclusiveness in falsification.<br><br>**Popper's Premise**: While no finite set of observations can conclusively verify a hypothesis, a single negative observation (or series of observations) can conclusively confute or falsify it (modus tollens: $H \to O$, $\neg O \vdash \neg H$).<br><br>**Ayer's Refutation (Unstated Assumption & Logic)**: A hypothesis cannot be conclusively confuted any more than it can be conclusively verified. When we take an observation $\neg O$ as proof that a hypothesis $H$ is false, we presuppose the existence of auxiliary conditions and background assumptions ($C$). The deduction is actually $(H \land C) \to O$. If $\neg O$ occurs, we are logically forced to conclude $\neg(H \land C)$, which is $\neg H \lor \neg C$. It is always logically possible and consistent to adjust or abandon one of the auxiliary hypotheses (denying $C$) to preserve the core hypothesis $H$. Since no hypothesis can be definitively confuted in absolute isolation, falsifiability fails as a conclusive criterion.<br><br>**Citation**: Chapter 1, Page 38.
