Why does Alfred Ayer classify the traditional philosophical attempt to provide an _a priori_ logical justification for scientific induction as a fictitious pseudo-problem?
#### Notes
**Nature of Propositions**: Dissolution of Pseudo-Problem & Category Error.<br><br>**Dialectical Counter-Argument**: Rationalists and skeptics demand an _a priori_ logical proof or guarantee for the validity of inductive generalizations and common-sense beliefs.<br>**Premise 1**: All genuine propositions are strictly divided into analytic tautologies (logically necessary, but devoid of factual content) and synthetic empirical hypotheses (factually informative, but intrinsically probable and fallible).<br>**Premise 2**: Scientific laws are synthetic empirical hypotheses whose sole justification is their success in predicting future experience.<br>**Refutation of Formal/Empirical Proofs**:<br>1. Induction cannot be deduced from formal tautologies, because a factual conclusion cannot follow from tautological premises.<br>2. Induction cannot be proved by empirical principles (such as the "uniformity of nature" or "limited independent variety"), because such principles merely re-state or presuppose the validity of past experience as a guide to the future, begging the question.<br>**Conclusion**: Demanding an _a priori_ guarantee of certainty for synthetic hypotheses is a category error—demanding logical necessity where only probability is obtainable. Thus, the traditional "problem of induction" is a fictitious problem.<br>**Citation**: Chapter 2, Pages 48–50.
