Why are probabilistic hypotheses mathematically impossible to strictly refute or confirm via Modus Tollens, and how do scientists practically evaluate them?
#### Notes
**Nature of Proposition: Epistemological Argument.** A probabilistic hypothesis like $p(A,D) = 0.15$ does not logically imply any specific outcome in a finite series of trials. Therefore, a large deviation from $0.15$ does not deductively falsify it, nor does a perfect match deductively prove it. Instead, the hypothesis implies that large departures from $0.15$ in long runs are statistically highly improbable. If observed long-run relative frequencies deviate severely from the hypothesized value, the hypothesis is rendered highly improbable and is practically refuted and rejected. (_Philosophy of Natural Science_, Chapter 5, pp. 64-65)
