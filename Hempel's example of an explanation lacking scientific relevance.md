How does Francesco Sizi's historical argument against the existence of Jupiter's satellites illustrate a failure of the requirement of explanatory relevance? How do explanations of gravitation in terms of "natural appetites" or "universal affinity" fail the requirement of testability, and what is their explanatory status?
#### Notes
**Nature of Proposition: Historical Case Example.** Sizi argued that because there are seven windows in the head (eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth), there must necessarily be exactly seven planets in the heavens, and because Jupiter's satellites are invisible to the naked eye, they can have no influence on Earth, making them useless and non-existent. Even if these premises were accepted without question, they are entirely irrelevant to the existence of the satellites, providing no logical grounds to expect them not to exist. (_Philosophy of Natural Science_, Chapter 5, p. 48)


**Nature of Proposition: Epistemological Argument.** Explaining gravitational attraction as a manifestation of an underlying "natural tendency akin to love or hatred" has no test implications whatsoever. Because no conceivable empirical finding could ever bear out or disconfirm this claim, it is devoid of empirical content and lacks objective explanatory power, functioning as a pseudo-explanation similar to appeals to an "inscrutable fate." (_Philosophy of Natural Science_, Chapter 5, p. 49 / p. 239)
