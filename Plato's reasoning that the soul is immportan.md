How does Socrates logically prove the immortality of the soul using the relationship between "good," "bad," and "specific evils"? (Book 10, 608e–611a). 
#### Notes
**Argued Position.**<br>• _Premise_: The "good" is what saves and benefits, while the "bad" is what corrupts and destroys (608e).<br>• _Premise_: Every existing thing has a specific, natural evil or disease connected with it (e.g., ophthalmia for eyes, rust for iron, rot for wood) which corrupts and eventually dissolves it (608e–609a). If a thing's own specific evil cannot destroy it, then nothing else (certainly not the good, which only preserves) can destroy it (609a–b).<br>• _Premise_: The soul's specific, natural evil is injustice and vice (609b–c).<br>• _Argument_: Experience shows that injustice does not dissolve or destroy the soul; indeed, the most unjust tyrants are often exceptionally vital and sleepless (610d–e). Since the soul's own specific evil (injustice) cannot kill or dissolve it, and since an alien evil (like physical disease or death of the body) cannot introduce the soul's specific evil to destroy it, it is argued that the soul cannot be destroyed by any evil, meaning it must be immortal (Book 10, 609d–611a).
