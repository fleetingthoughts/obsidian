How does Socrates prove that imitative art appeals to an inferior, non-rational part of the soul by exploiting optical illusions? (Book 10, 602c–603a)
#### Notes
**Argued Position.**<br>• _Premise_: Human sight is easily deceived by optical illusions (e.g., a straight stick appearing bent in water, or objects looking unequal from different distances) (602c).<br>• _Premise_: The soul's **calculating part** (_logistikon_) resolves these illusions through measuring, counting, and weighing, which represent the work of the best part of the soul (602d–e).<br>• _Premise_: It is psychologically impossible for the same part of the soul to hold contrary opinions about the same thing at the same time (602e).<br>• _Argument_: When the calculating part measures and shows that things are equal, but the eye still perceives them as unequal, the soul experiences contrary opinions simultaneously. Therefore, the part of the soul that trusts visual appearances must be distinct from, and far inferior to, the calculating part. Since imitative art thrives on visual appearances, it is argued to keep company strictly with this non-rational, inferior part of our nature (Book 10, 602e–603a).
