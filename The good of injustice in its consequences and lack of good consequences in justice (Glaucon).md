State Glaucon's position on the value of Justice with respect to the consequential life lead by a just man and unjust man and what motivates him to make this argument
#### Notes
**Part 3: The Unjust Life vs. The Just Life (The Two Statues) (360e–362c)**
- **Methodological Premise:** To judge the life of these two men correctly, we must set the most just man and the most unjust man in direct opposition, taking each as perfect in his pursuit (360e–361a).
- **The Perfectly Unjust Man:** Like a clever pilot or doctor, he attempts unjust deeds correctly and gets away with them while maintaining the ultimate reputation for justice (360e–361b). If he trips up, he has the persuasive speech, courage, strength, friends, and money to set himself aright (361a–b).
- **The Perfectly Just Man:** A simple and noble man stripped of everything except justice itself (361b–c). He is given the greatest reputation for injustice so his justice may be tested against a bad reputation and its consequences until death (361c–d). He is whipped, racked, bound, has his eyes burned out, and is finally crucified (361e–362a).
- **Argument / Conclusion:** The unjust man, pursuing reality rather than mere opinion, rules in the city, marries whomever he wants, secures profitable partnerships, and makes magnificent sacrifices to the gods (362a–c). Thus, both gods and men provide a better life for the unjust man than for the just man (362c).
