How does Socrates use the metaphor of the sea-god Glaucus to explain why the soul's true nature is obscured in bodily life? (Book 10, 611b–612a)
#### Notes
**Premise.** Socrates establishes the metaphor that the soul, as we observe it in mortal life, is heavily maimed and covered in seaweed, shells, and rocks grown from its constant feasting on the earth—resembling the unrecognizable, wave-battered body of the sea-god Glaucus (611c–d). To perceive the soul's true, unmaimed nature (whether it is single-formed or many-formed), we must look to its **love of wisdom** (_philosophia_), examining what it lays hold of and longs to keep company with (the divine, immortal, and eternal) when it is raised out of the deep, earthy ocean of physical life (Book 10, 611d–612a).
