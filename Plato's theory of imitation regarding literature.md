What distinction does Socrates make regarding the method of speeches and its impact on one's virtue
#### Notes
Human nature is minted in such "small coins" (395b) that a man cannot imitate many things well while remaining a craftsman of the city's freedom (394e–395b). Socrates deconstructs _mimesis_ as a threat to the "oneness" of the guardian’s nature. The rule of specialization—"one man, one job"—makes the "manifold" imitator a danger to the city’s structural integrity.

- **Key Propositions/Claims:**
    - Prohibition on imitating "base" roles (women, slaves, bad men, madmen) (395d–396a).
    - The "mixed" poet who imitates everything from thunder to dogs is to be expelled (397a–398a).
- **Assumed Premises:**
    - Imitations practiced from youth become established as "habits and nature" in body, sound, and thought (395d).
- **Argued Premises:**
    - The "Chryses" example: Homer "hides himself" by adopting the priest’s persona, showing how imitation bypasses the simple report (393a–394a).
    - The "small coin" argument: A soul cannot maintain metaphysical unity while imitating manifold objects (395a–b).
- **Connective Tissue:** The expulsion of the poet leads naturally to the non-verbal elements of education: music and rhythm.

Continuous practice of imitation (_mimesis_) from youth onwards becomes deeply established as second nature, shaping a person’s physical habits, vocal expressions, and underlying thought processes, potentially leading to the actual adoption of the base traits being imitated (Book 3, 395c–d).
