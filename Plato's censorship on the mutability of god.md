What foundational premises does Socrates make regarding the mutability of and its implications with respect to literature
#### Notes
The premise is that god is perfect and any changes to perfection makes it worse, therefore god is immutable. The consequence of this is the censorship of the tropes in literature where God's disguise themselves

**The Second Theological Model / Law: God is Immutable, Simple, and Non-Deceitful (380d–383c)**

- **Premise 1 (Immutability):** Things in the best condition (bodies, plants, implements, souls) are least altered or moved by external forces (380d–381a). Since God and divine things are in every way in the best condition, God least of all has many shapes (381b).
- **Premise 2:** No god or human voluntarily makes himself worse in beauty or virtue (381b–c).
- **Argument:** God cannot transform himself; each remains forever simply in his own shape (381c). Reject tales of shape-shifting gods (Proteus, Thetis, or Hera in disguise) (381c–e).
- **Premise 3 (Hatred of the True Lie):** All gods and humans hate the "true lie"—which is holding ignorance or falsehood in the soul regarding reality (382a–c). A lie in speeches is a phantom or imitation of the soul's affection, useful to humans as a preventive drug against enemies or mad friends, or when telling myths due to ignorance of ancient truth (382c–d).
- **Argument:** None of these conditions apply to God: God has no fear of enemies, no mad friends, and no ignorance of antiquity (382d–e).
- **Conclusion / Law:** God is completely simple and true in deed and speech, and does not change or deceive others by illusions, speeches, or dream signs (382e–383a). Reject Homer's story of Zeus sending the false dream to Agamemnon or Aeschylus' claims of Apollo lying (383a–c).
