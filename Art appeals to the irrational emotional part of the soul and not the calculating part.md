How does Socrates contrast the rational management of grief with the behavior encouraged by imitative poetry? (Book 10, 603e–604d)
#### Notes
**Argued Position.**<br>• _Premise_: When a decent man suffers a great loss (such as the death of his son), his soul experiences a conflict between the suffering that draws him to lament and the law/argument that commands him to keep quiet (603e–604a).<br>• _Premise_: The law commands restraint because human affairs are not worthy of great seriousness, and grieving prevents the soul from applying **"reason's medicine"**—which consists of calmly accepting the fall of the dice and deliberating how best to settle one's affairs (604b–d).<br>• _Argument_: The calculating part of the soul is willing to follow this rational ordering, whereas the irrational, idle part leads to endless reminiscence of suffering and lamentation (604d). Because this quiet, rational character is hard to imitate and difficult for a theater crowd to understand, imitative poets naturally target the irritable, irrational disposition, reinforcing a disordered psychology (Book 10, 604e–605a).
