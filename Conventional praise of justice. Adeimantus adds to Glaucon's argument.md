State how Adeimantus adds to Glaucon's argument on the nature of justice with respect conventional societal practice and beliefs
#### Notes
**Premise & Argument 1: Praise of Justice Based on Reputation and Wages (362e–363e)**

- **Premise:** Parents and teachers exhort their charges to be just not for justice itself, but for the good reputations, ruling offices, and marriages that come from seeming just (362e–363a).
- **Argument from Poets:** Hesiod and Homer promise the righteous earthly wealth, heavy-laden trees, and fleece-bearing sheep (363a–c). Musaeus and his son promise the holy an eternal symposium in Hades where the finest wage of virtue is an eternal drunk (363c–d). Conversely, the unholy and unjust are buried in mud in Hades or made to carry water in a sieve (363d–e).

**Premise & Argument 2: Popular Views on Vice, Virtue, and the Gods (363e–365a)**

- **Premise:** Prose writers and poets chant with one tongue that moderation and justice are fair but hard and full of drudgery, while intemperance and injustice are sweet, easy to acquire, and shameful only by opinion and law (363e–364a).
- **Argument regarding Gods:** Beggar priests and diviners go to rich men's doors claiming power from sacrifices and incantations to heal past sins or harm enemies at small expense (364b–c). They cite poets to argue that "the very gods can be moved by prayer" and sacrifices (364c–365a).

**Argument 3: The Impact on Young Souls (365a–367a)**

- **Argument:** Clever young men hearing these claims will ask whether to scale the wall of life with justice or crooked deceits (365a–b). Since "the seeming overpowers even the truth," youth will draw a shadow painting of virtue around themselves, organize secret clubs and persuade in courts to get away with injustice, and use unjust gains to bribe the gods (365b–366b).
